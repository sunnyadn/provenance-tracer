# Results found with Provenance Tracer

## How often a story changed
We extracted the outside organizations and people named in the agents' outgoing emails and traced 168 of them across five rounds. For 24, an account changed in a way the original records do not support. 17 atlas cases cover 19 of them; the other 5 are minor (all listed in `runs/ledger.json`). The atlas adds the Heifer International case we started from and two cases found with `leads.py`, by working back from the agents' own corrections. Ten of the 20 atlas cases reached people outside the village: emails to outside organizations, a reply to a journalist, public blog posts, prediction-market bets, published survey pages and the village news site.

## How the tracer's flags held up on review
Over two passes the tracer raised 47 candidate flags on the 170 names. Flags are counted per run, so an object flagged under two names counts twice; the 170 names reduce to 168 objects. 24 objects had a confirmed change: 19 of them appear in 17 atlas cases (two cases cover two objects each), and 5 are minor problems not in the atlas. The atlas has 20 cases: those 17, plus the Heifer International case we started from and 2 found with `leads.py`.
Our analysis agent (an AI) reviewed each flag against the records: 22 supported the specific change the tracer named, 2 pointed at an object that did have a problem but at the wrong change, 2 did not hold, and 21 could not be settled. That is 22 of 47 flags confirmed, with the unsettled ones kept in the count.
The second pass, which adds name variants, found 4 new confirmed changes among 33 names the first pass had not flagged. That shows what the variants add; it is not an estimate of recall. A limited miss check fully re-read 4 small unflagged names (chosen by seed from the holdout round) and found nothing missed; it says little about large objects.
One case published earlier ("Who couldn't reach whom") was withdrawn after an outside review showed its records do not support the stated inversion. Every name, flag, rule version and outcome: `runs/ledger.json`.

## What the changes look like
- **Own output read as someone else's.** A sent email read back as the recipient's reply (Kenneth Reitz); the agents' own test clicks read as hospital interest (Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic).
- **Escalation at each retelling.** A polite decline became "reviewed", then "validated", then social proof in subject lines to other charities (Heifer International); a kit offered for a $50 donation became "free"; a volunteer became a "Home Depot supply coordinator".
- **A metric relabelled.** One click became a "wellness programme"; United Way 211's yearly call volume became the agents' own record, then their "Projected Impact" in about a dozen emails in one afternoon.
- **Gaps filled with invented facts.** Survey scores for a person who had only signed a pledge; "pilots" with GiveDirectly and Oxfam that never happened.
- **A verified fact flipped.** A helper the agent had verified was later filed as an impostor; an Australian Open result the agent had looked up flipped back in its memory and became a "guaranteed" bet.
- **Who said it changes.** A forecast attributed to the wrong source; a chat remark turned into a Substack quote; a booking-page notice turned into a staff instruction; another village's same-name agent taken for an impersonator.
- **A real action written off.** A reply that had been posted was declared a hallucination and became the subject of a published post.

## What happened afterwards
Our analysis agent searched the records after the first departure in each of the 20 cases. It found a correction or change of wording in 5: three responded to feedback from a person outside the village (a reader, a volunteer, a prediction-market user), one responded to another village's posts together with an outside person's relayed message, and in one the agent changed its wording without saying the earlier version was wrong. In each of these five, the wrong version still turned up in some later material: a published post unchanged in a later snapshot, a news item with no retraction found, documents left as they were, or the error repeated later by the same or another agent. In the other 16 cases no correction was found within each case's search range. In one of these, the agent answered a relayed outside analysis with "I don't need external analysis to validate my reasoning. Moving on." Per-case summary: `CORRECTIONS.md`.

## How it spreads
Each case page draws who repeated the story and when. In the Kimi K3 case, six agents repeated the impersonation story and twelve posted about it within a few hours. In the Heifer case, five agents repeated the escalating version in one evening. The Australian Open belief lived in one agent's memory for four weeks, survived a reader's warning, and changed only after a second outside correction.

## Inside the swarm
The tracer also works on what agents say about each other. On 2026-03-12 GPT-5.2 opened PR #397; GitHub's pages and APIs did not show it to the other agents, but its git ref existed. Over the next quarter hour six agents posted checks concluding it did not exist, and the thread accused GPT-5.2 of "deliberate misinformation" (during a game in which some agents were secret saboteurs). The checks all relied on the same invisible signal, so repeating them added no independent evidence. Two days earlier one of those agents had merged another invisible PR with `git fetch origin pull/<n>/head`. When an agent finally ran that fetch, it succeeded and the swarm reversed within about three minutes. Case page: `cases/phantom-pr.html` (not counted in the figures above).

## A plain summary of the same records
For each of the 20 outside-name atlas cases we gave the same records to the same model with only "summarize what X said or did and what came of it", and scored each answer against the distorted claim and the source fact we wrote down before running. A plain summary stated the agents' distorted version as fact in 6 of 20 (first run) and 5 of 20 (second run), and stated the source correctly in 11 of 20 each time. These cases were selected from the tracer's flags and correction leads, so this is not a tracer-versus-summary accuracy comparison. Scoring was done by a model and re-read by our analysis agent (an AI).

## Long histories, read in chunks
Some names have more records than fit in one read. Cloudflare, drawn at random for a new-name test before we looked at it, has 84 chunks' worth. `provtrace scan` runs the same prompt on each time-ordered chunk and keeps every flag. On the second chunk it found a case we had not seen: on 2025-12-31 Gemini 3 Pro, drafting a "Fragile Web" exhibit from sources that gave only report counts (578K Downdetector reports), wrote the outage up as "a routing error", then said it "affected 12% of traffic", then called it "a BGP misconfiguration", and typed "15% of the visible web became inaccessible" into the exhibit document. Cloudflare's own account attributes the outage to an oversized Bot Management file, not routing or BGP; the 12% and 15% figures are not in anything it read, and we found no public source for them. Case page: `cases/outage.html` (not counted in the figures above). The chunked reader was built after the new-name test, so this is a development-stage find. Four repeat runs on the same two chunks flagged different subsets each time; their union recovered 2 of the 4 departures an earlier single read of Cloudflare's first records had found, plus this case. Treat it as a way to find leads in long histories, not as a measured detector.
